## Perfect powers among squarefree tau values | 7 | Number theory
W: Ramanujan's tau function is the sequence of coefficients of the discriminant modular form. Show that, at squarefree arguments n > 1, |tau(n)| is a perfect square, cube or higher power only finitely many times (no example exists for n up to one million).
M: Let $\tau(n)$ be defined by $q\prod_{m\ge1}(1-q^m)^{24}=\sum_{n\ge1}\tau(n)q^n$. Prove that the set of squarefree $n>1$ for which $|\tau(n)|=y^k$ for some integers $y\ge 0$, $k\ge2$ is finite. (Computation: for all $2\le n\le 10^6$, squarefree or not, $|\tau(n)|$ is not a perfect power; squarefree is imposed because $\tau(p)=0$ would force $\tau(p^4)=p^{22}$.)

## Is Ramanujan's tau one-to-one? | 8 | Number theory
W: Show that two different positive integers never have the same nonzero Ramanujan tau value. For all n up to one million, even the absolute values |tau(n)| are pairwise distinct.
M: Prove that if $m\neq n$ are positive integers with $\tau(m)\tau(n)\neq0$, then $\tau(m)\neq\tau(n)$; more strongly, that $|\tau(m)|\neq|\tau(n)|$. (Verified: the values $|\tau(n)|$, $1\le n\le10^6$, are pairwise distinct.)

## Sign changes of tau along consecutive primes | 7 | Number theory
W: List the primes in order and record the sign of Ramanujan's tau at each one. Show that the sign flips between consecutive primes exactly half the time in the limit, so the signs carry no memory of the gap between neighbouring primes. Among primes below one million the flip rate is 0.5003.
M: Let $p_n$ be the $n$-th prime and $\varepsilon_n=\operatorname{sgn}\tau(p_n)$ (with $\operatorname{sgn}0=0$). Prove $\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac1N\#\{n\le N:\ \varepsilon_n\varepsilon_{n+1}=-1\}=\tfrac12$. More generally, show that the pairs $(\theta_{p_n},\theta_{p_{n+1}})$, where $\tau(p)=2p^{11/2}\cos\theta_p$, become equidistributed with respect to the product of two Sato–Tate measures. (Data: over the $78497$ consecutive pairs of primes below $10^6$ the flip rate is $0.50029$, and it stays within $0.02$ of $\tfrac12$ when conditioned on each gap size $\le 30$ that occurs at least $1000$ times. For consecutive integers $n,n+1$ the log-averaged version follows from Tao's two-point Elliott theorem, since $\operatorname{sgn}\tau$ is multiplicative and non-pretentious; along consecutive primes no such tool applies.)

## Square point counts on the conductor-11 curve | 7 | Number theory
W: Take the elliptic curve of smallest conductor, y² + y = x³ − x² − 10x − 20, and count its points modulo each prime p. Show that this count is a perfect square for infinitely many primes, and that up to x this happens roughly √x / log x times. There are 236 such primes below one million, starting 23, 31, 101, 109.
M: Let $E=11a1$ and $N_p=\#E(\mathbb F_p)=p+1-a_p$, where $\sum a_nq^n=q\prod_{m\ge1}(1-q^m)^2(1-q^{11m})^2$. Prove that $\{p\ne 11: N_p\text{ is a perfect square}\}$ is infinite. More precisely, show that its counting function is $\asymp \sqrt x/\log x$. (Since $5\mid N_p$ for $p\ne 5, 11$, every square value is divisible by $25$. Verified: exactly $236$ primes $p<10^6$, $p\neq 11$, have $N_p$ a square, starting $23,31,101,109,373$. For CM curves such as $y^2=x^3-x$ the analogous set reduces to primes represented by a polynomial in three variables; the non-CM case has no such structure.)

## Squarefree partition numbers | 8 | Number theory
W: Show that a positive proportion of the partition numbers p(n) are squarefree. Ramanujan's congruences make p(n) divisible by 25, 49 or 121 along whole progressions, so the proportion should be noticeably below the 61% that random integers have. Among n up to 6000, about 50% of p(n) have no square factor ℓ² with ℓ < 20000.
M: Let $p(n)$ be the partition function. Prove $\liminf_{x\to\infty}\frac1x\#\{n\le x:\ p(n)\text{ squarefree}\}>0$. Further, show the density exists and equals $\prod_\ell(1-\delta_\ell)$, where $\delta_\ell$ is the density of $n$ with $\ell^2\mid p(n)$ (existence of each $\delta_\ell$ is part of the claim). (Data: for $1\le n\le 6000$, a fraction $0.505$ of the $p(n)$ are not divisible by $\ell^2$ for any prime $\ell<2\cdot10^4$, against $6/\pi^2\approx0.608$ for random integers.)

## The reciprocal tau series | 8 | Number theory
W: Add up 1/|tau(n)| over all n where Ramanujan's tau is nonzero. Show that this series converges. To do that you must rule out |tau(p)| being very small, for example equal to 2, for infinitely many primes p. Up to one million the partial sum is 1.04679, and |tau(n)| ≥ 12n throughout that range.
M: Prove that $\sum_{n\ge1,\ \tau(n)\ne0}\frac1{|\tau(n)|}<\infty$. Since $\sum 1/|\tau(n)|=\prod_p\sum_{k\ge0,\,\tau(p^k)\ne0}|\tau(p^k)|^{-1}$, this amounts to $\sum_p\big(|\tau(p)|^{-1}+|\tau(p^2)|^{-1}\big)<\infty$ over the nonzero terms. That in turn requires, for instance, that $|\tau(p)|\le p^{1-\delta}$ holds only on a set of primes of convergent reciprocal sum, which is far beyond effective Sato–Tate in shrinking windows. (Data: $\sum_{n\le10^6}1/|\tau(n)|=1.046787\ldots$, and $|\tau(n)|\ge 12n$ for $2\le n\le10^6$, with equality only at $n=2$.)

## The two sophomore's dream constants | 8 | Number theory
W: The "sophomore's dream" identities give the integral of x^(−x) from 0 to 1 as the sum of n^(−n), and the integral of x^x as the alternating version of that sum. Show that at least one of these two numbers (1.29129 and 0.78343) is irrational.
M: Let $A=\int_0^1x^{-x}\,dx=\sum_{n\ge1}n^{-n}$ and $B=\int_0^1x^{x}\,dx=\sum_{n\ge1}(-1)^{n+1}n^{-n}$. Prove that $A$ and $B$ are not both rational; equivalently, that $\sum_{m\ge1}(2m)^{-2m}$ and $\sum_{m\ge0}(2m+1)^{-(2m+1)}$ are not both rational. (The sequence $n^{-n}$ is not P-recursive, so neither number is a known E- or G-function value, and Apéry- or Siegel–Shidlovskii-type methods do not apply directly. The natural stronger goal is that $1,A,B$ are linearly independent over $\mathbb Q$.)

## The continued fraction of the squares | 7 | Number theory
W: The continued fraction with partial quotients 1, 2, 3, 4, … is a ratio of Bessel function values, and it is known to be transcendental. Show that the continued fraction with partial quotients 1, 4, 9, 16, … (the squares; value 0.80432) is transcendental too. A first step would be to show it is not an algebraic number of degree 3.
M: Let $\xi=[0;1,4,9,16,\dots]=[0;a_1,a_2,\dots]$ with $a_n=n^2$, so $\xi=0.8043185611\ldots$. Prove that $\xi$ is transcendental. (For $a_n=n$, $[0;1,2,3,\dots]=I_1(2)/I_0(2)$ is transcendental by Siegel. For $a_n=n^2$ the convergent denominators satisfy $q_n=n^2q_{n-1}+q_{n-2}$ and grow like $c\,(n!)^2$; neither $\sum q_nx^n/(n!)^2$ nor any evident normalization is an E- or G-function. Also, $a_n$ grows too slowly for Liouville or subspace-theorem criteria based on rapidly growing partial quotients.) A first goal: prove that $\xi$ is not algebraic of degree $3$.

## Ramanujan's near-integers are finite | 8 | Number theory
W: Numbers like e^(π√163) are absurdly close to integers because of class number one and the j-function. Show that e^(π√n) is within e^(−π√n/2) of an integer only for n = 2, 6, 37, 58, 67 and 163. Also show that, for every fixed c > 0, being within e^(−cπ√n) of an integer happens only finitely often.
M: For a non-square positive integer $n$ write $\|e^{\pi\sqrt n}\|=e^{-\rho(n)\pi\sqrt n}$. (a) Prove that $\{n:\rho(n)>\tfrac12\}=\{2,6,37,58,67,163\}$. (b) Prove that $\{n:\rho(n)>c\}$ is finite for every $c>0$. (Computation at 400-digit precision, all non-square $n<6000$: $\rho(n)>0.3$ only for $n=2,6,7,17,18,22,37,43,58,67,163$, with $\rho(163)=0.696$, $\rho(58)=0.650$, $\rho(6)=0.610$, $\rho(37)=0.561$, $\rho(67)=0.526$, $\rho(43)=0.408$. The near-integers come from CM points of small class number via $j$ and Weber functions. Linear forms in logarithms give only lower bounds of the rough shape $e^{-C\pi\sqrt n\log n}$, far too weak for (b).)

## Tau(p) modulo p is uniform | 7 | Number theory
W: For each prime p, reduce Ramanujan's tau(p) modulo p and divide by p, giving a point of [0, 1). Show that these points are uniformly distributed as p runs over the primes. This would give a heuristic reason why p divides tau(p) so rarely (only p = 2, 3, 5, 7, 2411 below one million). The test statistic for the primes below one million matches a uniform sample.
M: Prove that the sequence $\{\tau(p)/p\}$, with $p$ running over primes in increasing order, is equidistributed modulo $1$. (Note that $|\tau(p)|<2p^{11/2}$ is far larger than $p$, so this is a fine-scale statement about $\tau(p)$ and is not implied by Sato–Tate. Verified: for the $78498$ primes $p<10^6$, the Kolmogorov–Smirnov distance of $\{(\tau(p)\bmod p)/p\}$ from uniform is $0.0027$, i.e. $\sqrt{n}D=0.76$. A consequence of a quantitative form, $\#\{p\le x:\tau(p)\equiv0\bmod p\}\sim\log\log x$, is the Lang–Trotter-type prediction for non-ordinary primes, which this problem does not claim.)

## Truncations of the discriminant form are irreducible | 5 | Number theory
W: Cut off the q-expansion of the discriminant modular form after n terms, giving the polynomial tau(1) + tau(2)x + … + tau(n)x^(n−1). Show that every such polynomial is irreducible over the rationals, in the spirit of Schur's theorem on truncated exponential series. This holds for every n up to 740.
M: For $N\ge2$ let $T_N(x)=\sum_{k=1}^{N}\tau(k)x^{k-1}\in\mathbb Z[x]$. Prove that $T_N$ is irreducible over $\mathbb Q$ for every $N\ge2$, and determine its Galois group (conjecturally $S_{N-1}$ for all $N\ge 3$). (Verified with FLINT factorization: $T_N$ is irreducible for $2\le N\le740$. The same holds for the partition truncations $\sum_{k=0}^{N}p(k)x^k$ with $1\le N\le300$. Natural tools are $\ell$-adic Newton polygons combined with Ramanujan's congruences, e.g. $\tau(n)$ is odd exactly when $n$ is an odd square, and $\tau(n)\equiv\sigma_{11}(n)\pmod{691}$.)

## Powers of e avoid integers | 8 | Number theory
W: Show that e^n is never extremely close to an integer. Precisely: for every ε > 0, the distance from e^n to the nearest integer exceeds e^(−εn) once n is large. Up to n = 4000, the distance is below e^(−n/10) only for n ≤ 29, and it is below e^(−n/30) for no n ≥ 100.
M: Write $\|e^n\|=e^{-\rho_n n}$. Prove that $\rho_n\to0$, i.e. $\log\|e^n\|=o(n)$. A first target is $\limsup\rho_n<1$, i.e. $\|e^n\|\ge e^{-(1-\delta)n}$ for large $n$. (Computed at $2300$-digit precision for $1\le n\le4000$: the largest values are $\rho_1=1.27$, $\rho_3=0.82$, $\rho_2=0.47$, $\rho_8=0.40$; $\max_{n\ge20}\rho_n=\rho_{29}=0.109$; $\max_{n\ge100}\rho_n=\rho_{135}=0.033$. Known transcendence measures for $e$ give only super-exponentially small lower bounds for $\|e^n\|$. This is the analogue for $e$ of Mahler's question on $\|(3/2)^n\|$, but for a transcendental base, where the integers $\lfloor e^n\rfloor$ carry no evident arithmetic structure.)

## Fine-scale statistics of Frobenius angles | 9 | Number theory
W: For the elliptic curve of conductor 11, each prime p has a Frobenius angle, and the angles follow the Sato–Tate law. Stretch the angles so that this law becomes uniform. Show that the gaps between neighbouring stretched angles then follow the exponential law of random points, with no repulsion as there is for zeros of L-functions. The data up to one million fit this well. The one anomaly is a slowly fading pile-up near the right angle, coming from primes with a_p = 0 and from primes with small a_p.
M: Let $E=11a1$, $a_p=2\sqrt p\cos\theta_p$ with $\theta_p\in[0,\pi]$, and $u_p=F(\theta_p)$ where $F(\theta)=(\theta-\sin\theta\cos\theta)/\pi$ is the Sato–Tate distribution function. For $x$ large let $N=\#\{p\le x\}$ and consider the set $U_x=\{u_p:p\le x,\ p\ne11\}$. Prove that for every fixed $s>0$, $\frac1N\#\{(p,q):p\ne q\le x,\ 0\le u_q-u_p<s/N\}\to s$ (Poissonian pair correlation), and that the normalized nearest-neighbour spacings converge in distribution to $\mathrm{Exp}(1)$. (Data, $p<10^6$, $N=78497$: the fraction of normalized spacings below $s=0.25,0.5,1,2$ is $0.2216,0.3913,0.6319,0.8669$, against $1-e^{-s}=0.2212,0.3935,0.6321,0.8647$. The pair correlation at $s=1$ is $1.054$ but at $s=0.1$ it is $1.59$. This excess comes from the supersingular primes (conjecturally $\asymp\sqrt x/\log x$ of them), which all have $u_p=\tfrac12$, and from primes with small $|a_p|$; in the limit it should decay like $1/\log x$. Proving that decay already requires $\#\{p\le x: a_p=0\}=o(\sqrt{x/\log x})$, well beyond current upper bounds for supersingular primes.)

## Square values of truncated exponentials | 6 | Number theory
W: Cut the series for e^x after the x^n/n! term. Show that for every n ≥ 5 the only rational x at which this polynomial takes a rational square value is x = 0. For n = 3 and n = 4 the curves are elliptic and do have other points, such as x = 8/3 and x = −8/5.
M: Let $e_n(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}x^k/k!$ and $C_n: y^2=e_n(x)$, a curve of genus $\lfloor (n-1)/2\rfloor$. Prove that for every $n\ge5$, $C_n(\mathbb Q)$ consists of $(0,\pm1)$ together with the points at infinity. (Search: for $5\le n\le10$, no rational $x=a/b\neq0$ with $1\le b\le150$, $|a|\le900$ makes $e_n(x)$ a rational square. For $n=3$ the search finds $x=-3/2,\,8/3,\,75/8$; for $n=4$ it finds $x=-8/5,\,312/23$. Each fixed $n$ might be handled by Chabauty methods if the Jacobian rank is small. The problem asks for a proof uniform in $n$, which would exploit the Schur–Coleman structure of $e_n$: it is irreducible, its Galois group is $A_n$ or $S_n$, and it has strong $p$-adic Newton polygons.)

## Poisson gaps between Kloosterman angles | 8 | Number theory
W: Fix a large prime p and compute the p − 1 Kloosterman sums K(a, 1; p). Katz showed their angles follow the Sato–Tate law. Show that, after stretching the angles so that law becomes uniform, the gaps between neighbouring angles follow the exponential law of independent random points. This fits the data at p = 19997 and p = 99991.
M: For a prime $p$ and $a\in\mathbb F_p^\times$ put $K(a)=\sum_{x\in\mathbb F_p^\times}e\big((x+a\bar x)/p\big)=2\sqrt p\cos\theta_a$, $\theta_a\in[0,\pi]$, and $u_a=(\theta_a-\sin\theta_a\cos\theta_a)/\pi$. Prove that as $p\to\infty$ the normalized nearest-neighbour spacings $(p-1)(u_{(i+1)}-u_{(i)})$ of the sorted $u_a$ converge in distribution to $\mathrm{Exp}(1)$, and that the pair correlation of $\{u_a\}$ is Poissonian. (Data, computed by FFT: the fraction of spacings below $s=0.1,0.25,0.5,1,2$ is $0.0924,0.2212,0.3976,0.6341,0.8654$ at $p=19997$ and $0.0947,0.2209,0.3930,0.6327,0.8641$ at $p=99991$, against $1-e^{-s}=0.0952,0.2212,0.3935,0.6321,0.8647$; no two of the angles coincide. Deligne–Katz equidistribution controls test functions of bounded degree; spacings at scale $1/p$ need Chebyshev degrees of order $p$, where the error term $O(k\sqrt p)$ is useless.)

## Signs of Kloosterman sums at a fixed prime | 7 | Number theory
W: For a fixed prime p, about half of the p − 1 Kloosterman sums K(a, 1; p) are positive. Show that the number of positive ones minus the number of negative ones is at most about √p, up to small factors, rather than the p^(3/4) that current equidistribution bounds give. In tests the excess is typically only 0.4√p.
M: With $K(a)=\sum_{x\in\mathbb F_p^\times}e((x+a\bar x)/p)$, let $S(p)=\sum_{a\in\mathbb F_p^\times}\operatorname{sgn}K(a)$. Prove $S(p)\ll_\varepsilon p^{1/2+\varepsilon}$. (Niederreiter's discrepancy bound $O(p^{-1/4})$ for Kloosterman angles gives only $S(p)\ll p^{3/4}$. Data: over the $29$ primes in $[10007,10200)\cup[100003,100100)$, $S(p)/\sqrt p$ has mean $-0.03$, root-mean-square $0.40$ and maximum absolute value $0.94$. The root-mean-square is well below the value $1$ for independent random signs, which suggests correlations among the signs that a proof could exploit.)
