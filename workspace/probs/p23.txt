## Perfect powers among squarefree tau values | 7 | Number theory
W: Ramanujan's tau function is the sequence of coefficients of the discriminant modular form. Show that, at squarefree arguments n > 1, |tau(n)| is a perfect square, cube or higher power only finitely many times (no example exists for n up to one million).
M: Let $\tau(n)$ be defined by $q\prod_{m\ge1}(1-q^m)^{24}=\sum_{n\ge1}\tau(n)q^n$. Prove that the set of squarefree $n>1$ for which $|\tau(n)|=y^k$ for some integers $y\ge 0$, $k\ge2$ is finite. (Computation: for all $2\le n\le 10^6$, squarefree or not, $|\tau(n)|$ is not a perfect power; squarefree is imposed because $\tau(p)=0$ would force $\tau(p^4)=p^{22}$.)

## Is Ramanujan's tau one-to-one? | 8 | Number theory
W: Show that two different positive integers never have the same nonzero Ramanujan tau value. For all n up to one million, even the absolute values |tau(n)| are pairwise distinct.
M: Prove that if $m\neq n$ are positive integers with $\tau(m)\tau(n)\neq0$, then $\tau(m)\neq\tau(n)$; more strongly, that $|\tau(m)|\neq|\tau(n)|$. (Verified: the values $|\tau(n)|$, $1\le n\le10^6$, are pairwise distinct.)

## Sign changes of tau along consecutive primes | 7 | Number theory
W: List the primes in order and record the sign of Ramanujan's tau at each one. Show that the sign flips between consecutive primes exactly half the time in the limit, so the signs carry no memory of the gap between neighbouring primes. Among primes below one million the flip rate is 0.5003.
M: Let $p_n$ be the $n$-th prime and $\varepsilon_n=\operatorname{sgn}\tau(p_n)$ (with $\operatorname{sgn}0=0$). Prove $\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac1N\#\{n\le N:\ \varepsilon_n\varepsilon_{n+1}=-1\}=\tfrac12$. More generally, show that the pairs $(\theta_{p_n},\theta_{p_{n+1}})$, where $\tau(p)=2p^{11/2}\cos\theta_p$, become equidistributed with respect to the product of two Sato–Tate measures. (Data: for the $78497$ primes below $10^6$ the flip rate is $0.50029$, and it stays within $0.02$ of $\tfrac12$ when conditioned on each gap size $\le 30$ that occurs at least $1000$ times. For consecutive integers $n,n+1$ the log-averaged version follows from Tao's two-point Elliott theorem, since $\operatorname{sgn}\tau$ is multiplicative and non-pretentious; along consecutive primes no such tool applies.)

## Square point counts on the conductor-11 curve | 7 | Number theory
W: Take the elliptic curve of smallest conductor, y² + y = x³ − x² − 10x − 20, and count its points modulo each prime p. Show that this count is a perfect square for infinitely many primes, and that up to x this happens roughly √x / log x times. There are 236 such primes below one million, starting 23, 31, 101, 109.
M: Let $E=11a1$ and $N_p=\#E(\mathbb F_p)=p+1-a_p$, where $\sum a_nq^n=q\prod_{m\ge1}(1-q^m)^2(1-q^{11m})^2$. Prove that $\{p\ne 11: N_p\text{ is a perfect square}\}$ is infinite. More precisely, show that its counting function is $\asymp \sqrt x/\log x$. (Since $5\mid N_p$ for $p\ne 5, 11$, every square value is divisible by $25$. Verified: exactly $236$ primes $p<10^6$, $p\neq 11$, have $N_p$ a square, starting $23,31,101,109,373$. For CM curves such as $y^2=x^3-x$ the analogous set reduces to primes represented by a polynomial in three variables; the non-CM case has no such structure.)

## Squarefree partition numbers | 8 | Number theory
W: Show that a positive proportion of the partition numbers p(n) are squarefree. Ramanujan's congruences make p(n) divisible by 25, 49 or 121 along whole progressions, so the proportion should be noticeably below the 61% that random integers have. Among n up to 6000, about 50% of p(n) have no square factor ℓ² with ℓ < 20000.
M: Let $p(n)$ be the partition function. Prove $\liminf_{x\to\infty}\frac1x\#\{n\le x:\ p(n)\text{ squarefree}\}>0$. Further, show the density exists and equals $\prod_\ell(1-\delta_\ell)$, where $\delta_\ell$ is the density of $n$ with $\ell^2\mid p(n)$ (existence of each $\delta_\ell$ is part of the claim). (Data: for $1\le n\le 6000$, a fraction $0.505$ of the $p(n)$ are not divisible by $\ell^2$ for any prime $\ell<2\cdot10^4$, against $6/\pi^2\approx0.608$ for random integers.)

## The reciprocal tau series | 8 | Number theory
W: Add up 1/|tau(n)| over all n where Ramanujan's tau is nonzero. Show that this series converges. To do that you must rule out |tau(p)| being very small, for example equal to 2, for infinitely many primes p. Up to one million the partial sum is 1.04679, and |tau(n)| ≥ 12n throughout that range.
M: Prove that $\sum_{n\ge1,\ \tau(n)\ne0}\frac1{|\tau(n)|}<\infty$. Since $\sum 1/|\tau(n)|=\prod_p\sum_{k\ge0,\,\tau(p^k)\ne0}|\tau(p^k)|^{-1}$, this amounts to $\sum_p\big(|\tau(p)|^{-1}+|\tau(p^2)|^{-1}\big)<\infty$ over the nonzero terms. That in turn requires, for instance, that $|\tau(p)|\le p^{1-\delta}$ holds only on a set of primes of convergent reciprocal sum, which is far beyond effective Sato–Tate in shrinking windows. (Data: $\sum_{n\le10^6}1/|\tau(n)|=1.046787\ldots$, and $|\tau(n)|\ge 12n$ for $2\le n\le10^6$, with equality only at $n=2$.)

## The two sophomore's dream constants | 8 | Number theory
W: The "sophomore's dream" identities give the integral of x^(−x) from 0 to 1 as the sum of n^(−n), and the integral of x^x as the alternating version of that sum. Show that at least one of these two numbers (1.29129 and 0.78343) is irrational.
M: Let $A=\int_0^1x^{-x}\,dx=\sum_{n\ge1}n^{-n}$ and $B=\int_0^1x^{x}\,dx=\sum_{n\ge1}(-1)^{n+1}n^{-n}$. Prove that $A$ and $B$ are not both rational; equivalently, that $\sum_{m\ge1}(2m)^{-2m}$ and $\sum_{m\ge0}(2m+1)^{-(2m+1)}$ are not both rational. (The sequence $n^{-n}$ is not P-recursive, so neither number is a known E- or G-function value, and Apéry- or Siegel–Shidlovskii-type methods do not apply directly. The natural stronger goal is that $1,A,B$ are linearly independent over $\mathbb Q$.)

## The continued fraction of the squares | 7 | Number theory
W: The continued fraction with partial quotients 1, 2, 3, 4, … is a ratio of Bessel function values, and it is known to be transcendental. Show that the continued fraction with partial quotients 1, 4, 9, 16, … (the squares; value 0.80432) is transcendental too. A first step would be to show it is not an algebraic number of degree 3.
M: Let $\xi=[0;1,4,9,16,\dots]=[0;a_1,a_2,\dots]$ with $a_n=n^2$, so $\xi=0.8043185611\ldots$. Prove that $\xi$ is transcendental. (For $a_n=n$, $[0;1,2,3,\dots]=I_1(2)/I_0(2)$ is transcendental by Siegel. For $a_n=n^2$ the convergent denominators satisfy $q_n=n^2q_{n-1}+q_{n-2}$ and grow like $c\,(n!)^2$; neither $\sum q_nx^n/(n!)^2$ nor any evident normalization is an E- or G-function. Also, $a_n$ grows too slowly for Liouville or subspace-theorem criteria based on rapidly growing partial quotients.) A first goal: prove that $\xi$ is not algebraic of degree $3$.

## Ramanujan's near-integers are finite | 8 | Number theory
W: Numbers like e^(π√163) are absurdly close to integers because of class number one and the j-function. Show that e^(π√n) is within e^(−π√n/2) of an integer only for n = 2, 6, 37, 58, 67 and 163. Also show that, for every fixed c > 0, being within e^(−cπ√n) of an integer happens only finitely often.
M: For a non-square positive integer $n$ write $\|e^{\pi\sqrt n}\|=e^{-\rho(n)\pi\sqrt n}$. (a) Prove that $\{n:\rho(n)>\tfrac12\}=\{2,6,37,58,67,163\}$. (b) Prove that $\{n:\rho(n)>c\}$ is finite for every $c>0$. (Computation at 400-digit precision, all non-square $n<6000$: $\rho(n)>0.3$ only for $n=2,6,7,17,18,22,37,43,58,67,163$, with $\rho(163)=0.696$, $\rho(58)=0.650$, $\rho(6)=0.610$, $\rho(37)=0.561$, $\rho(67)=0.526$, $\rho(43)=0.408$. The near-integers come from CM points of small class number via $j$ and Weber functions. Linear forms in logarithms give only lower bounds of the rough shape $e^{-C\pi\sqrt n\log n}$, far too weak for (b).)
