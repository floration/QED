## Do two permutations commute? | 4 | Computation
W: Alice holds one permutation of n items and Bob holds another. How many bits must they exchange, using shared randomness and allowing a small error, to decide whether the two permutations commute? A simple reduction from set disjointness shows about n bits are needed, and sending a whole permutation costs about n log n bits. Find the right answer.
M: For $\pi,\sigma\in S_n$ let $\mathrm{COMM}_n(\pi,\sigma)=1$ iff $\pi\sigma=\sigma\pi$, with $\pi$ given to Alice and $\sigma$ to Bob, and let $R(\mathrm{COMM}_n)$ be its public-coin randomized communication complexity with error $1/3$. Splitting $[n]$ into blocks $\{3j,3j+1,3j+2\}$, with Alice applying $(3j\ 3j{+}1)$ iff $x_j=1$ and Bob applying $(3j{+}1\ 3j{+}2)$ iff $y_j=1$, embeds disjointness on $\lfloor n/3\rfloor$ bits, so $\Omega(n)\le R(\mathrm{COMM}_n)\le O(n\log n)$. Determine $R(\mathrm{COMM}_n)$ up to a constant factor. In particular, decide whether $R(\mathrm{COMM}_n)=o(n\log n)$.

## Does the product of two permutations fix a point? | 4 | Computation
W: Alice holds a permutation π and Bob holds a permutation σ of n items. They want to know whether π after σ leaves some item where it was. A three-item gadget turns set disjointness into this problem, so about n bits are needed. Is about n bits always enough, or do they really need n log n?
M: Let $\mathrm{FIX}_n(\pi,\sigma)=1$ iff there is $i\in[n]$ with $\pi(\sigma(i))=i$. On each block of three points, Alice using $c$ if her bit is 1 and $\mathrm{id}$ if it is 0, and Bob using $c^{-1}$ if his bit is 1 and $c$ if it is 0, where $c$ is a 3-cycle (so the block contains a fixed point of $\pi\sigma$ exactly when both bits are 1), embeds disjointness, giving $R(\mathrm{FIX}_n)=\Omega(n)$, while $R(\mathrm{FIX}_n)=O(n\log n)$ is trivial. Determine whether $R(\mathrm{FIX}_n)=\Theta(n)$, where $R$ is public-coin randomized communication complexity with error $1/3$.

## Information-tight sorting of pattern classes | 5 | Computation
W: Suppose an array is promised to avoid some fixed pattern (for example, no four entries in the relative order 1, 3, 2, 4). Counting the allowed orders gives a lower bound on the number of comparisons needed to sort it, and Fredman's theorem says this bound plus 2n comparisons always suffice. Is the counting bound always tight up to a negligible error, for every forbidden pattern?
M: For a permutation $\tau$ let $\mathrm{Av}_n(\tau)$ be the set of $\tau$-avoiding permutations of $[n]$ and let $C_\tau(n)$ be the least worst-case number of comparisons $a_i\overset{?}{<}a_j$ needed by a decision tree that determines the order of $a_1,\dots,a_n$ when the order pattern of $(a_i)$ is promised to lie in $\mathrm{Av}_n(\tau)$. Fredman's bound gives $\log_2|\mathrm{Av}_n(\tau)|\le C_\tau(n)\le\log_2|\mathrm{Av}_n(\tau)|+2n$. Decide whether $C_\tau(n)=\log_2|\mathrm{Av}_n(\tau)|+o(n)$ for every $\tau$. In particular, decide this for $\tau=1324$, whose growth rate is unknown.

## Erdős–Szekeres by comparisons | 4 | Computation
W: Any list of k² + 1 distinct numbers contains k + 1 numbers that rise or fall in order. How many comparisons between entries does it take to actually find such a run? Patience sorting uses about N log k comparisons for a list of N = k² + 1 numbers. Can it be done with a constant number of comparisons per entry?
M: Let $N=k^2+1$ and let $T(k)$ be the least worst-case number of comparisons $a_i\overset{?}{<}a_j$ needed by a deterministic algorithm that, given distinct reals $a_1,\dots,a_N$, outputs indices $i_1<\dots<i_{k+1}$ with $(a_{i_t})$ monotone. Patience sorting gives $T(k)=O(N\log k)$. Decide whether $T(k)=O(N)$ or $T(k)=\Theta(N\log k)$. Also determine the randomized (expected-comparison) complexity.

## Egyptian fractions with k terms | 5 | Computation
W: Given a fraction a/b and a number k, can a/b be written as a sum of k different unit fractions 1/x? Find out how hard this decision is. Even membership in NP is unclear, because the denominators can need exponentially many digits in k.
M: Let $\mathrm{EGYPT}=\{(a,b,1^k): \exists\,x_1<\dots<x_k\in\mathbb Z_{>0},\ a/b=\sum_{i=1}^k 1/x_i\}$, with $a,b$ in binary and $k$ in unary. Determine its complexity. Specifically: (i) is $\mathrm{EGYPT}\in\mathrm{NP}$? (ii) is it NP-hard, possibly under randomized reductions? (iii) is the restriction to $k=3$ decidable in time polynomial in $\log b$ given an oracle for integer factorization?

## Distances in four-peg Hanoi | 5 | Computation
W: In Tower of Hanoi with four pegs, the fewest moves between the two "all discs on one peg" positions is now known (Bousch, 2014). Give a polynomial-time method to find the fewest moves between any two legal positions, or show none exists under standard assumptions.
M: Let $H^4_n$ be the state graph of the Tower of Hanoi with 4 pegs and $n$ discs: vertices are $\{1,2,3,4\}^n$ (peg of each disc), and edges are legal single-disc moves. Is there an algorithm computing $d_{H^4_n}(u,v)$ for arbitrary $u,v\in\{1,2,3,4\}^n$ in time $\mathrm{poly}(n)$? (For 3 pegs this is linear time; for 4 pegs no subexponential algorithm is known to the author.)

## Fewest presses in Lights Out | 4 | Computation
W: In Lights Out on an n × n board, pressing a cell flips it and its four neighbours. Given a pattern, find the fewest presses that turn every light off. Solving is linear algebra, but the solutions form a family of size 2^d, and d can be a large fraction of n. Is there a polynomial-time method for the fewest presses?
M: Let $A_n=T_n\otimes I+I\otimes T_n+I$ over $\mathbb F_2$, where $T_n$ is the adjacency matrix of the path $P_n$. Given $n$ and $b\in\mathbb F_2^{n^2}$ in the column space of $A_n$, compute $\min\{|x|:A_nx=b\}$ (Hamming weight). Is this computable in time $\mathrm{poly}(n)$? The nullity $d(n)=\dim\ker A_n=\deg\gcd(p_n(x),p_n(x+1))$, with $p_0=1,\ p_1=x,\ p_{k+1}=xp_k+p_{k-1}$, is large for some $n$: computed $d(527)=350$ and $d(495)=320$. So brute force over the kernel takes time $2^{\Theta(n)}$ for infinitely many $n$.

## Smallest zero-one multiple | 5 | Computation
W: Every n has a multiple written only with the digits 0 and 1, with at most n digits. Can the shortest such multiple be found in time polynomial in the number of digits of n? Or is deciding whether one exists with at most d digits NP-hard?
M: For $n\ge1$ coprime to 10 let $L(n)$ be the least number of digits of a positive multiple of $n$ whose decimal digits lie in $\{0,1\}$, i.e. the least $d$ with $\sum_{i\in S}10^i\equiv0\pmod n$ for some nonempty $S\subseteq\{0,\dots,d-1\}$. Decide whether the language $\{(n,1^d):L(n)\le d\}$ ($n$ in binary) is NP-hard under randomized polynomial-time reductions, or whether $L(n)$ can be computed in time $\mathrm{poly}(\log n)$.

## Sorting by swap-or-rotate | 4 | Computation
W: You can rearrange a row of n cards only by swapping the first two or by moving the first card to the end (or the reverse). Given an arrangement, find the fewest such moves that sort it. Is this NP-hard, or is there a polynomial-time formula?

Determine the complexity of computing $d_{\Gamma_n}(\mathrm{id},\pi)$ for $\pi\in S_n$ given in one-line notation. Is the decision version ($d_{\Gamma_n}(\mathrm{id},\pi)\le k$?) ## Finding Huang's vertex | 6 | Computation
W: Huang's theorem says any set of more than half the corners of an n-dimensional cube contains a corner with at least √n neighbours in the set. When the set is given by a small circuit, how hard is it to find such a corner? Place this search problem among the known classes of total search problems.
M: $\mathrm{HUANG}$: given a Boolean circuit $f:\{0,1\}^{n}\to\{0,1\}^n$ whose domain is read as $[2^{n-1}+1]$ (inputs beyond $2^{n-1}+1$ are ignored), output either $i\ne j$ with $f(i)=f(j)$, or indices $i,j_1,\dots,j_m$ with $m=\lceil\sqrt n\rceil$ such that $f(j_1),\dots,f(j_m)$ are distinct and each is at Hamming distance 1 from $f(i)$. By Huang's theorem and the pigeonhole principle this is a TFNP problem. Is $\mathrm{HUANG}\in\mathrm{FP}$? Is it in $\mathrm{PPP}$, $\mathrm{PPA}$ or $\mathrm{PLS}$, and is it complete for any standard subclass of TFNP?

## Roots modulo almost every prime | 6 | Computation
W: Some polynomials, like (x² − 2)(x² − 3)(x² − 6), have a root modulo almost every prime without having a rational root. Can a computer decide this property in time polynomial in the size of the polynomial? It reduces to a question about the Galois group, and no polynomial-time algorithm for computing Galois groups is known.
M: Let $\mathcal I=\{f\in\mathbb Z[x]: f \text{ has a root modulo } p \text{ for all but finitely many primes } p\}$, with $f$ given by its degree in unary and its coefficients in binary. By Chebotarev, $f\in\mathcal I$ iff every element of $G=\mathrm{Gal}(f)$ fixes a root of $f$, so $\mathcal I$ is decidable. Is $\mathcal I\in\mathrm P$? If not known, is it at least in $\mathrm{NP}$ or $\mathrm{coNP}$ unconditionally? (Under GRH, effective Chebotarev places the complement in NP.)

## The Mandelbrot set at Gaussian rationals | 7 | Computation
W: Is there an algorithm that takes a complex number with rational real and imaginary parts and decides whether it lies in the Mandelbrot set? Escaping points can be confirmed and attracting cycles can be found. But some Gaussian rationals, such as c = (37 + 16i)/100, lie on the boundary with an irrationally rotating fixed point, and other kinds of boundary point might hide there too.
M: Let $M=\{c\in\mathbb C:\ (f_c^{n}(0))_{n\ge0}\text{ bounded}\}$, $f_c(z)=z^2+c$. Prove or disprove that $M\cap\mathbb Q(i)$ is a decidable subset of $\mathbb Q(i)$. (The parameter $c=\mu/2-\mu^2/4$ with $\mu=(3+4i)/5$ equals $(37+16i)/100\in\partial M$; its fixed point has multiplier $\mu$, which is not a root of unity. Every element of $\mathbb Q(i)\setminus\mathbb Z[i]$ is neither a centre nor a Misiurewicz parameter, since those are algebraic integers.)

## Kolakoski in polylogarithmic time | 6 | Computation
W: The Kolakoski sequence 1, 2, 2, 1, 1, 2, 1, 2, 2, 1, … is its own run-length list. Its n-th term can be computed in logarithmic memory and about n steps. Can it be computed in time polynomial in the number of digits of n?

## Recamán's sequence, faster | 5 | Computation
W: Recamán's sequence starts at 0 and at step n goes back by n if that lands on a new positive number, and otherwise goes forward by n. Computing the n-th term seems to need simulating all earlier steps. Is there an algorithm using substantially fewer than n steps?
M: Let $a_0=0$ and $a_n=a_{n-1}-n$ if $a_{n-1}-n>0$ and $a_{n-1}-n\notin\{a_0,\dots,a_{n-1}\}$, otherwise $a_n=a_{n-1}+n$. Is there an algorithm that computes $a_n$ (on a word-RAM with $O(\log n)$-bit words) in time $O(n^{1-\varepsilon})$ for some fixed $\varepsilon>0$? ## Hamiltonian cycles of the grid are #P₁-complete | 7 | Computation
W: The number of closed tours through every cell of an n × n grid can be computed in time exponential in n by transfer matrices. Show that no polynomial-time method exists unless every counting problem with unary input is easy. In complexity terms, show that this count is complete for #P₁.
M: Let $h(n)$ be the number of Hamiltonian cycles of the grid graph $P_n\times P_n$ (with $n$ given in unary). Prove that $h$ is $\#\mathrm P_1$-complete under polynomial-time Turing reductions, or give an algorithm computing $h(n)$ in time $\mathrm{poly}(n)$.

## Integer complexity is NP-hard | 6 | Computation
W: The integer complexity of n is the fewest 1s needed to write n using +, × and brackets. Deciding whether it is at most k is in NP, since a short formula is a certificate. Is the problem NP-hard for n written in binary, or can it be solved in time polynomial in the number of digits?
M: Let $\|n\|$ be the least number of $1$'s in an expression for $n$ built from $1$, $+$, $\times$ and parentheses. Since $\|n\|\le3\log_2 n+O(1)$, the language $\{(n,k):\|n\|\le k\}$ with $n$ in binary lies in NP. Prove that it is NP-hard (possibly under randomized reductions), or show that $\|n\|$ can be computed in time $(\log n)^{O(1)}$.

## How hard is the hat guessing number? | 5 | Computation
W: Each person at a vertex of a graph gets a hat in one of q colours. Each sees only the neighbours' hats and guesses their own colour, and the team wins if someone is always right. Deciding whether a team on a given graph can win with 3 colours is a finite problem. How hard is it?
M: For a graph $G$ and $q\ge2$, a strategy is a family of maps $g_v:[q]^{N(v)}\to[q]$ ($v\in V(G)$). It wins if for every colouring $c:V\to[q]$ some $v$ has $g_v(c|_{N(v)})=c(v)$. Let $\mathrm{HG}(G)$ be the largest $q$ with a winning strategy. Determine the complexity of deciding $\mathrm{HG}(G)\ge3$ for an arbitrary input graph $G$. Is it NP-hard? Is it in PSPACE, or is it $\mathrm{NEXP}$-hard? (For graphs of bounded maximum degree it lies in $\Sigma_2^{\mathrm P}$.)

## Proof complexity of Graham–Pollak | 5 | Logic
W: The edges of a complete graph on n vertices cannot be split into fewer than n − 1 complete bipartite pieces. The known proofs use linear algebra. Written as a propositional formula, does this fact have short proofs in resolution, in cutting planes, or in low-degree sum-of-squares?
M: Let $\mathrm{GP}_n$ be the CNF with variables $\ell_{t,v},r_{t,v}$ ($t\in[n-2]$, $v\in[n]$) and auxiliary variables $e_{t,uv}\leftrightarrow(\ell_{t,u}\wedge r_{t,v})\vee(r_{t,u}\wedge\ell_{t,v})$, with clauses $\neg\ell_{t,v}\vee\neg r_{t,v}$, and for each edge $uv$ of $K_n$ the clauses saying exactly one $e_{t,uv}$ is true. $\mathrm{GP}_n$ is unsatisfiable by the Graham–Pollak theorem and has size $\mathrm{poly}(n)$. Determine whether $\mathrm{GP}_n$ has (a) resolution refutations of size $2^{n^{o(1)}}$, (b) polynomial-size cutting-planes refutations, (c) sum-of-squares refutations of degree $O(1)$.

## Proof complexity of the sensitivity theorem | 6 | Logic
W: Huang proved that any set of more than half the corners of the n-cube contains a corner with at least √n neighbours inside the set. Write this as a propositional formula with one variable per corner. Do polynomial-size proofs exist in resolution, cutting planes or sum-of-squares, measured against the 2^n variables?
M: Let $N=2^n$, $d=\lceil\sqrt n\rceil-1$, and let $\mathrm{SENS}_n$ be the unsatisfiable CNF on variables $x_v$ ($v\in\{0,1\}^n$) plus auxiliary counter variables expressing $\sum_v x_v\ge 2^{n-1}+1$ (sequential-counter encoding), together with, for every $v$ and every $(d+1)$-set $U$ of neighbours of $v$, the clause $\neg x_v\vee\bigvee_{u\in U}\neg x_u$. Does $\mathrm{SENS}_n$ have refutations of size $N^{O(1)}$ in (a) resolution, (b) cutting planes, (c) sum-of-squares (or degree $O(\log N)$ sum-of-squares)? ## Three Hadamard rows | 4 | Logic
W: There are no three ±1 rows of length n, with n twice an odd number, that are pairwise orthogonal. The reason is a short counting argument modulo 4. Encode this as a propositional formula. Do resolution or cutting planes need exponentially long proofs of it?
M: For $n\equiv2\pmod4$ let $\mathrm{HAD3}_n$ be the CNF on variables $x_{r,j}$ ($r\in\{1,2,3\}$, $j\in[n]$) with auxiliary variables $z_{rs,j}\leftrightarrow x_{r,j}\oplus x_{s,j}$ and sequential-counter clauses asserting $\sum_j z_{rs,j}=n/2$ for each pair $r<s$. Prove that resolution refutations of $\mathrm{HAD3}_n$ require size $2^{n^{\Omega(1)}}$, and determine whether cutting-planes refutations of size $n^{O(1)}$ (or $n^{O(\log n)}$) exist.

## Steiner triple systems in polynomial calculus | 4 | Logic
W: A Steiner triple system on n points exists only when n leaves remainder 1 or 3 on division by 6. The impossibility is a divisibility count: modulo 2 for even n, modulo 3 when n leaves remainder 5. Show that algebraic proofs working in the wrong characteristic need high degree.
M: Let $\mathrm{STS}_n$ be the polynomial system over a field $\mathbb F$ in variables $x_T$, $T\in\binom{[n]}3$: $x_T^2=x_T$, $\sum_{T\supset\{a,b\}}x_T=1$ and $x_Tx_{T'}=0$ for distinct $T,T'\supset\{a,b\}$ (all pairs $\{a,b\}$). It has no $0/1$ solution unless $n\equiv1,3\pmod 6$. For $n\equiv5\pmod6$ it has a degree-1 Polynomial Calculus refutation over $\mathbb F_3$. Prove that over $\mathbb F_2$ every refutation needs degree $n^{\Omega(1)}$. Symmetrically, for even $n$ the system is refuted in low degree over $\mathbb F_2$: prove a degree lower bound $n^{\Omega(1)}$ over $\mathbb F_3$.

## Highways of generalized Langton ants | 7 | Logic
W: A generalized Langton ant on a grid with k colours turns left or right according to a fixed rule word, steps the cell's colour forward, and moves on. Starting on a blank grid, some rules build a periodic "highway", some grow symmetric blobs forever, and some look chaotic. Show that no algorithm can tell, from the rule word, whether a highway eventually appears.
M: For $w\in\{L,R\}^k$ ($k\ge2$) the ant on $\mathbb Z^2$ starts at the origin facing north with all cells coloured $0$. At each step, on a cell of colour $c$ it turns by $w_c$, sets the colour to $c+1\bmod k$, and moves one cell forward. Say $w$ builds a highway if there are $t_0$, $p>0$ and $v\ne0$ such that for all $t\ge t_0$ the position at time $t+p$ is the position at time $t$ plus $v$, the heading at time $t+p$ equals the heading at time $t$, and the colours seen at times $t+p$ and $t$ agree. Prove that $\{w:\ w\text{ builds a highway}\}$ is undecidable, or show it is decidable.

## Computable connective constants | 6 | Computation
W: Count the self-avoiding walks of length n on the Cayley graph of a finitely generated group. Their n-th root tends to the connective constant, and upper bounds converge to it. For groups with a solvable word problem, is the connective constant always a computable number?
M: Let $G$ be a finitely generated group with solvable word problem and finite symmetric generating set $S$, let $c_n$ be the number of $n$-step self-avoiding walks from $e$ in the Cayley graph $\mathrm{Cay}(G,S)$, and let $\mu(G,S)=\lim c_n^{1/n}=\inf_n c_n^{1/n}$ (so $\mu$ is upper semicomputable). ## Logic of random Latin squares | 6 | Logic
W: Pick a Latin square of order n uniformly at random and view it as a multiplication table on n symbols. Does every first-order statement about it have a limiting probability as n grows? A full zero-one law should fail. For example, the chance that some x satisfies x·x = x presumably tends to 1 − 1/e.

Prove or disprove the first-order convergence law: for every first-order sentence $\varphi$ in the language $\{\circ\}$, $\lim_{n\to\infty}\Pr[L_n\models\varphi]$ exists. As a first step, prove that $\Pr[\exists x\ (x\circ x=x)]\to1-e^{-1}$.
