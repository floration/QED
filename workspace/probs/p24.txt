## Area-bisector polygon map | 4 | Geometry
W: From each corner of a convex polygon draw the line that cuts its area in half, and join the points where these lines leave the polygon to get a new polygon. Repeating this does not make polygons regular: tested pentagons up to nonagons all freeze, after about a hundred steps, into irregular shapes that the map only stretches and shears. Prove this always happens, and find how many free parameters the frozen shapes have.
M: For a convex $n$-gon $P=v_1\cdots v_n$ ($n\ge5$) let $w_i\neq v_i$ be the second boundary point of the unique line through $v_i$ halving the area of $P$, and $B(P)=\mathrm{conv}(w_1,\dots,w_n)$ (the $w_i$ are in cyclic order). Prove that for every convex $n$-gon the affine classes of $B^k(P)$ converge as $k\to\infty$, the limit $Q$ satisfying $B(Q)=A(Q)$ for an affine map $A$ (up to a cyclic shift of labels). Determine the dimension of the set of such affine classes $Q$ inside the $(2n-6)$-dimensional space of affine classes of $n$-gons. (Numerics, $5\le n\le9$, random starts: convergence to non-affine-regular limits that depend on the start, so this set has positive dimension.)

## Most uniform chords | 5 | Geometry
W: Cut a convex shape by a random line. The disc seems to make the chord length vary least compared with its average: its ratio of mean square chord to squared mean chord is 32/(3π²) ≈ 1.081, against 1.132 for the hexagon, 1.205 for the square and 1.336 for the triangle. Prove no convex shape beats the disc.
M: For a planar convex body $K$ with perimeter $L$ and area $A$, let $\sigma$ be the chord length of a random line under the motion-invariant measure conditioned on meeting $K$, so $\mathbb E\sigma=\pi A/L$ and $\mathbb E\sigma^2=\frac1L\iint_{K\times K}\frac{dx\,dy}{|x-y|}$. Prove $L\iint_{K\times K}|x-y|^{-1}dx\,dy\ \ge\ \tfrac{32}{3}A^2$, with equality only for discs. (Numerically checked for regular 3-, 4-, 6-gons and for small $\cos 2\theta$, $\cos3\theta$ radial perturbations of the disc. Riesz rearrangement and the isoperimetric inequality push in opposite directions.)

## Rolling polygons | 6 | Geometry
W: Take a polygon and, going round its corners in order, reflect each corner across the line through its two neighbours; side lengths never change, so the shape tumbles across the plane. Quadrilaterals march off in a straight line, but tested pentagons, hexagons and heptagons wander far more slowly, roughly like a random walk. Prove that for five or more sides the drift is slower than linear, and decide whether it is genuinely diffusive.
M: For a planar closed polygon $(v_1,\dots,v_n)$ apply, for $s=1,2,\dots$ with $i\equiv s\pmod n$, the move $v_i\mapsto$ reflection of $v_i$ in the line $v_{i-1}v_{i+1}$ (a half-turn bending in the sense of Kapovich–Millson); it preserves all side lengths. Let $c_N$ be the vertex centroid after $N$ moves. For $n=4$, $|c_N|/N$ tends to a nonzero limit for generic starts. Prove that for $n\ge5$ and Lebesgue-almost every initial polygon, $c_N/N\to0$. Decide whether $|c_N-c_0|=N^{1/2+o(1)}$ for almost every start. (Tested: $n=5,6,7$, a few random near-regular starts, $N\le3\cdot10^5$: displacements $\approx10^2$ at $N=3\cdot10^5$, versus $\approx0.17N$ for $n=4$.)

## Equal-area realizations of polyhedra | 5 | Geometry
W: Can every type of convex polyhedron, specified by which faces meet which, be built so that all its faces have exactly the same area? Pyramids, prisms and tetrahedra can. Find a polyhedron type that cannot be, or show all can.
M: Does every 3-connected planar graph $G$ have a realization as a convex 3-polytope whose 2-faces all have equal area? If not, characterize the graphs that do. (The realization space modulo similarities has dimension $e(G)-1$ and equal areas impose $f(G)-1$ conditions, so the expected dimension is $e-f=v-2$; decide whether every nonempty equiareal realization space has this dimension.)

## Cubic unit distances | 6 | Geometry
W: Call two points a cubic pair if the second is reached from the first by a step of the form (t, t³). Among n points in the plane, how many cubic pairs can there be? The general theory caps it at about n^(4/3), while a stretched grid gives about n^(5/4). Find the true exponent.
M: Let $\Gamma=\{(t,t^3):t\in\mathbb R\setminus\{0\}\}$ and $U(n)=\max_{|P|=n}\#\{(p,q)\in P^2:\ q-p\in\Gamma\}$. Translates of $\Gamma$ pairwise meet in at most two points and form a two-parameter family, so $U(n)=O(n^{4/3})$; the set $\{1,\dots,N\}\times\{1,\dots,N^3\}$ gives $U(n)=\Omega(n^{5/4})$. Determine $\lim \log U(n)/\log n$, or at least prove $U(n)=O(n^{4/3-c})$ for some $c>0$, or $U(n)=\Omega(n^{5/4+c})$. (For the parabola $(t,t^2)$ the analogous count is $\Theta(n^{4/3})$, as in Valtr's strictly convex norms; the question is whether the curvature-vanishing inflection of $t^3$ at the origin and its cubic growth change the exponent.)

## Only the sphere is of negative type | 6 | Geometry
W: Distances measured along the surface of a round ball pass Schoenberg's "negative type" test, one of the basic conditions for a distance to come from a sum of cut-and-measure pieces. Distances along the surface of a cube fail it, and so do distances on a flat doubled disc. Show that the round sphere is the only convex surface whose surface distances pass.
M: For a convex body $K\subset\mathbb R^3$ let $d_K$ be the intrinsic (geodesic) metric of $\partial K$. Call $d_K$ of negative type if $\sum_{i,j}b_ib_j\,d_K(x_i,x_j)\le0$ for all finite $x_i\in\partial K$ and real $b_i$ with $\sum b_i=0$. Round spheres qualify ($d_K$ is even $L^1$-embeddable, by Crofton's formula). Prove that if $d_K$ is of negative type then $K$ is a ball. Allow degenerate (doubly covered planar) $K$. (Computed: for $84$ points on the unit cube surface, including the vertices, the matrix $(I-\tfrac1mJ)D(I-\tfrac1mJ)$ has eigenvalue $0.366>0$; for $120$ random points on a doubly covered unit disc it has eigenvalue $3.0>0$.) Is the same true with negative type replaced by $L^1$-embeddability, and does some neighbourhood of the round sphere already fail?

## How round is the cube's surface? | 6 | Geometry
W: Distances along the surface of a cube can be copied into a straight-line space with every distance stretched or shrunk by a factor of at most D. A belt around the cube is an exact circle, which forces D to be at least π/2, while the cube's own position in space gives D = 2. Find the best D, and decide whether it is exactly π/2.
M: Let $d$ be the intrinsic metric on the surface $S$ of the unit cube and $c_2(S)=\inf_f \mathrm{Lip}(f)\,\mathrm{Lip}(f^{-1})$ over injective $f:S\to\ell_2$. The equatorial belt is isometric to a circle of length $4$, so $c_2(S)\ge\pi/2$, and the inclusion $S\subset\mathbb R^3$ has distortion $2$ (opposite face centres). Determine $c_2(S)$; in particular decide whether $c_2(S)=\pi/2$. (Checked numerically: on 400 random pairs of belt points the surface distance equals the belt distance.) Determine also the value for the regular octahedron.

## Fattest cyclic polytopes | 4 | Geometry
W: Put n points on the curve (t, t², t³) with t between −1 and 1, both endpoints included, and choose them to make the convex hull as big as possible. In the plane, on the parabola, equal spacing is best. In three and four dimensions the best points crowd toward the ends, and they are not the zeros of any classical orthogonal polynomial. Find the limiting density of the best points.
M: For $d\ge3$ and $n>d$ let $V_d(n)=\max \mathrm{vol}_d\,\mathrm{conv}\{\gamma(t_1),\dots,\gamma(t_n)\}$ over $-1=t_1<\dots<t_n=1$, with $\gamma(t)=(t,t^2,\dots,t^d)$. Prove that the empirical measures of the maximizing nodes converge weakly to a density $\rho_d$ on $[-1,1]$, determine $\rho_d$ (it is uniform for $d=2$), and find the exponent $\alpha_d$ in $\mathrm{vol\,conv}\,\gamma([-1,1])-V_d(n)\asymp n^{-\alpha_d}$. (Numerical optima for $d=3,4$, $n\le8$ cluster at the ends, e.g. $d=3,n=8$: $\pm0.7667,\pm0.4978,\pm0.1785$, and they match no Gauss–Jacobi node set. The deficit is not a sum of local gap terms, because by Gale's evenness condition facets join non-adjacent nodes.)

## Equilateral associahedra | 5 | Geometry
W: The permutohedron and the cube can be built with every edge the same length. Can the associahedron, the polytope whose vertices are the bracketings of a product, also be built as a convex polytope with all edges equal, in every dimension?
M: Does the $d$-dimensional associahedron $K_{d+2}$ have, for every $d\ge3$, a convex realization in $\mathbb R^d$ (any realization with the correct face lattice) all of whose edges have the same length? Same question for the cyclohedron. If not, find the first $d$ that fails. The standard realizations (Loday, Chapoton–Fomin–Zelevinsky) are not equilateral.

## Triangle areas determine a polygon | 5 | Geometry
W: Write down the areas of all triangles formed by three corners of a convex polygon, as an unlabelled list. Does the list determine the polygon up to area-preserving affine maps? Prove this for generic polygons, and decide whether two genuinely different convex polygons can ever have the same list.
M: For a convex $n$-gon $P$ let $\mathcal A(P)$ be the multiset of the $\binom n3$ areas of triangles with vertices among those of $P$. Prove that for every $n\ge5$ there is a dense open set of convex $n$-gons $P$ such that $\mathcal A(Q)=\mathcal A(P)$ for a convex $n$-gon $Q$ implies $Q=g(P)$ for some affine $g$ with $|\det g|=1$. Decide whether there exist, for some $n$, convex $n$-gons $P,Q$ with $\mathcal A(P)=\mathcal A(Q)$ that are not equi-affinely equivalent. This is the area analogue of the Boutin–Kemper reconstruction of point sets from unlabelled distances, where non-reconstructible (homometric) examples exist.

## Chopping a polygon at random | 5 | Geometry
W: Cut a convex polygon along the line through two random points inside it, and keep the bigger piece. Repeat forever, only ever looking at the shape up to affine maps. The number of corners settles into a random steady state with average about 4.68. If instead you keep the piece containing a third random point, the average is about 4.36. Prove the steady states exist and find these averages exactly.
M: Given a convex polygon $P$, pick $x,y$ independent and uniform in $P$ and let $P'$ be the piece of $P\setminus\overline{xy}$ (cut along the full line $xy$) of larger area, or, in the second rule, the piece containing a third independent uniform point $z$. Both rules commute with affine maps, so they define Markov chains on affine classes of convex polygons. Prove each chain has a unique stationary distribution attracting every starting polygon, and compute the stationary mean number of vertices $\mu_{\max}$, $\mu_z$. (Simulation, $1.5\cdot10^4$ steps from the square: $\mu_{\max}\approx4.68$, $\mu_z\approx4.36$, both $\pm0.03$; vertex-count frequencies for $\mu_{\max}$: $3{:}0.074$, $4{:}0.371$, $5{:}0.389$, $6{:}0.139$, $7{:}0.025$. Keeping a uniformly random piece gives exactly $4$ by a counting argument; the question is what replaces that argument for these two rules.)

## Dropping a shape on graph paper | 5 | Geometry
W: Drop a convex shape of area a at a random spot and angle onto paper ruled into unit squares. To have the best chance of touching no line, a disc is best for small a. A wobble with four bumps beats the disc exactly when a > 225π/1024 ≈ 0.690. Show that the disc is the overall best right up to that point, and find the best shape beyond it.
M: For a planar convex body $K$ with width function $w$, the probability that a uniformly random rigid motion of $K$ meets no line of the grid $(\mathbb Z\times\mathbb R)\cup(\mathbb R\times\mathbb Z)$ is $p(K)=\frac1{2\pi}\int_0^{2\pi}(1-w(\theta))_+(1-w(\theta+\tfrac\pi2))_+\,d\theta$. Let $a^*=225\pi/1024$. A second-variation computation shows that the disc of area $a$ is a strict local maximizer of $p$ among convex bodies of area $a$ for $a<a^*$, and that the perturbation $h=r+\delta\cos4\theta$ of its support function increases $p$ for $a>a^*$. Prove that the disc is the global maximizer for every $a\le a^*$, and describe the maximizers for $a^*<a<1$. (Numerically, at $a\le0.7$ the disc beats the square and the regular hexagon and octagon.)

## Do plane sections determine a body? | 6 | Geometry
W: Slice a convex solid by a random plane and record the area of the cut. For chords of plane shapes, Mallows and Clark found different polygons with the same chord-length statistics. Can two genuinely different convex solids give exactly the same statistics of slice areas?
M: For a convex body $K\subset\mathbb R^3$ let $\mu_K$ be the law of $\mathrm{area}(K\cap H)$ when $H$ is drawn from the motion-invariant measure on planes, conditioned on $H\cap K\ne\emptyset$. Do there exist convex bodies $K,L$, not related by an isometry of $\mathbb R^3$ (reflections allowed), with $\mu_K=\mu_L$? If not in general, decide whether $\mu_K$ determines $K$ among convex polytopes, or among bodies of revolution. (In the plane the chord-length law does not determine a convex polygon, by Mallows–Clark. A direct analogue of their polygon construction is not available for sections.)

## Greedy convex position | 4 | Geometry
W: Throw random points into a disc one at a time and keep a point only if it stays in convex position with all points kept so far. Simulation gives 12, 23, 39 and 77 kept points after 100, 1000, 10000 and 100000 throws, suggesting growth like the cube root. Prove the cube-root law and find its constant.
M: Let $x_1,x_2,\dots$ be i.i.d. uniform in the unit disc $D$. Set $S_0=\emptyset$ and $S_t=S_{t-1}\cup\{x_t\}$ if $S_{t-1}\cup\{x_t\}$ is in convex position, else $S_t=S_{t-1}$. Prove that $|S_t|/t^{1/3}$ converges almost surely to a random variable $C\in(0,\infty)$ (it is random because the first kept points stay vertices forever and so shape the limiting curve $\partial\,\overline{\bigcup_t\mathrm{conv}\,S_t}$), and compute $\mathbb E\,C$ (simulation: $|S_{10^5}|=77$ in one run, so $C\approx1.7$). Describe the law of the limiting convex curve: is it almost surely $C^1$, and does it touch $\partial D$?

## Unit distances without scaling | 6 | Geometry
W: In the flat plane, the record numbers of equal distances come from scaled-up lattices. The hyperbolic plane has no scalings and no lattice of translations. How many pairs among n points of the hyperbolic plane can be exactly distance 1 apart? Is the answer just proportional to n, or does it grow faster?
M: Let $u_{\mathbb H}(n)$ be the maximum number of pairs at hyperbolic distance exactly $1$ among $n$ points of $\mathbb H^2$ (curvature $-1$). The crossing-lemma argument gives $u_{\mathbb H}(n)=O(n^{4/3})$. Decide whether $u_{\mathbb H}(n)/n\to\infty$. If so, find the order of growth, for instance whether $u_{\mathbb H}(n)=\Theta(n\log n)$ as for the Euclidean hypercube constructions, or $n^{1+c}$. The Euclidean devices (Minkowski sums of unit vectors, scaled lattices, number fields) have no direct analogue, because commuting hyperbolic isometries share an axis and orbit graphs of discrete groups have bounded degree.

## Which cones are of negative type? | 5 | Geometry
W: Roll a paper sector into a cone with total angle α at its tip and measure distances along the paper. The flat plane (α = 2π) passes Schoenberg's negative-type test, but sharp cones fail. In random tests the failures stop somewhere near α = 1.7π. Find the exact threshold, and decide whether it is 2π.
M: Let $C_\alpha$ be the Euclidean cone of total angle $\alpha>0$ with its intrinsic metric: for points $(r_1,\theta_1),(r_2,\theta_2)$ with angular separation $\varphi\in[0,\alpha/2]$, $d=\sqrt{r_1^2+r_2^2-2r_1r_2\cos\varphi}$ if $\varphi<\pi$ and $d=r_1+r_2$ otherwise. Determine the set $\mathcal N$ of $\alpha$ for which $C_\alpha$ is of negative type, i.e. $\sum b_ib_jd(x_i,x_j)\le0$ whenever $\sum b_i=0$. Is $\mathcal N=[2\pi,\infty)$? Computations with $300$–$700$ uniform random points of the unit-radius part: positive eigenvalues of $(I-\frac1mJ)D(I-\frac1mJ)$ (violations) for every tested $\alpha\le1.7\pi$, e.g. $0.015$ at $1.7\pi$; none found at $1.75\pi,1.8\pi,1.9\pi,2.5\pi,4\pi$. Points on a single circle $r=1$ never violate, for any $\alpha$, by a Fourier computation. Since $C_\alpha$ is scale-invariant, every convex polyhedron with a vertex of cone angle in $\mathbb R_{>0}\setminus\mathcal N$ fails negative type; this bears on whether only round spheres have negative-type surfaces.

## Are random polyhedra Rupert? | 6 | Geometry
W: A solid is Rupert if a hole can be drilled through it big enough for an identical copy to pass. The ball is not Rupert, and the first non-Rupert polyhedron, found in 2025, needed a delicate construction. Take the convex hull of n random points on a sphere. As n grows, does the chance that it is Rupert go to zero, to one, or to something in between?
M: Let $P_n=\mathrm{conv}\{X_1,\dots,X_n\}$ with $X_i$ i.i.d. uniform on $S^2$. Call a convex body $K$ Rupert if there are rigid motions $g,h$ such that the orthogonal projection of $gK$ onto the $xy$-plane fits in the interior of the projection of $hK$ onto that plane. Determine $\lim_{n\to\infty}\Pr[P_n\text{ is Rupert}]$, or prove it exists. Equivalently, decide whether near-spherical random polytopes typically have one shadow that fits strictly inside another; the margin available is of the order of the Hausdorff distance, about $\log n/n$, between $P_n$ and the unit ball, and the question is whether random fluctuations of the shadows at that scale are large enough.

## Hull of a random grid | 4 | Geometry
W: Keep each point of an n by n grid independently with probability p and take the convex hull of what is left. Unlike random points in a square, whose hull has about (8/3) log n corners, the number of corners stays bounded as n grows: about 6.28 on average for p = 1/2 and 8.16 for p = 1/4. Find the limiting average exactly as a function of p.
M: Let $S_{n,p}\subset\{0,\dots,n-1\}^2$ contain each point independently with probability $p\in(0,1)$, and $V_{n,p}$ the number of vertices of $\mathrm{conv}\,S_{n,p}$. Prove $\mathbb EV_{n,p}\to v(p)<\infty$. Find $v(p)$ in closed form, or as an explicit series over primitive vectors. Determine the asymptotics of $v(p)$ as $p\to0$ and as $p\to1$ (where $v\to4$). (Simulation, $n=120$, $3000$ samples: $v(1/2)\approx6.28\pm0.02$, $v(1/4)\approx8.16\pm0.03$; at $n=1000$ the means are stable.)

## The boundary cost of covering a disc | 6 | Geometry
W: To cover a huge disc of radius R with unit discs you need about (2π/(3√3))R² of them, from the honeycomb pattern. The rim forces extra discs, about cR more. Find the constant c, which measures how well a honeycomb covering can be bent to follow a curved edge.
M: Let $N(R)$ be the least number of closed unit discs covering a closed disc of radius $R$. Kershner's theorem gives $N(R)\sim\frac{2\pi}{3\sqrt3}R^2$. Prove that $c=\lim_{R\to\infty}\big(N(R)-\frac{2\pi}{3\sqrt3}R^2\big)/R$ exists and determine it, or at least determine whether $c$ equals the analogous constant $c_{\mathrm{line}}$ per unit length for covering a half-plane's edge strip, i.e. $c=2\pi c_{\mathrm{line}}$ with $c_{\mathrm{line}}=\lim_{H\to\infty}\lim_{L\to\infty}\big(N_{L,H}-\frac{2}{3\sqrt3}LH\big)/(2L)$, where $N_{L,H}$ is the least number of unit discs covering $[0,L]\times[0,H]$ (curvature of the rim then costs nothing at first order). For packings the boundary term is governed by the Oler–Groemer inequality; no covering analogue with a sharp constant is known.

## Is equal-area realizability hard? | 6 | Computation
W: Given the combinatorics of a convex polyhedron, decide whether it can be built with all faces of equal area. Realizing a polyhedron with prescribed shapes is often as hard as solving arbitrary polynomial inequalities. Show that this decision problem is that hard, or find an efficient test.
M: Let EQA be the problem: given a 3-connected planar graph $G$ (equivalently a combinatorial type of 3-polytope), decide whether $G$ has a convex realization in $\mathbb R^3$ all of whose 2-faces have equal area. Is EQA $\exists\mathbb R$-complete? Steinitz's theorem makes plain realizability trivial in dimension 3, so any hardness must come from the area constraints. Alternatively, show EQA is decidable in polynomial time by showing every $G$ is realizable or by giving a combinatorial criterion.

## Area-cutting circle maps | 6 | Geometry
W: From a point on the edge of a convex shape, draw the chord that cuts off one third of the area on its left, then repeat from the chord's far end. This walks round the boundary like a circle map. For the disc it is a plain rotation, but its average speed (rotation number) depends on the shape: 0.41465 for the disc, about 0.41651 for the square, 0.41667 = 5/12 for the triangle. Show that ellipses are the only shapes for which these maps never lock onto rational speeds.
M: For a planar convex body $K$ and $\lambda\in(0,\tfrac12)$ let $T_\lambda:\partial K\to\partial K$ send $x$ to the endpoint $y$ of the chord $xy$ such that the part of $K$ to the left of $\overrightarrow{xy}$ has area $\lambda|K|$. $T_\lambda$ is an orientation-preserving homeomorphism commuting with affine maps; let $\rho_\lambda(K)$ be its rotation number. For ellipses $T_\lambda$ is conjugate to a rotation and $\lambda\mapsto\rho_\lambda$ is strictly increasing. Prove the converse: if $\lambda\mapsto\rho_\lambda(K)$ is strictly increasing on $(0,\tfrac12)$, or equivalently $T_\lambda^q=\mathrm{id}$ whenever $\rho_\lambda(K)=p/q$, then $K$ is an ellipse. Also determine $\inf_K\rho_\lambda(K)$ and $\sup_K\rho_\lambda(K)$. The disc is not always extremal: at $\lambda=1/4$ the numerics give triangle $0.3333$, disc $0.3676$, square $0.3742$. (Values from $1500$–$3000$ iterations; for the triangle $\rho$ locks at $5/12$ for $\lambda$ near $0.335$.)

## Monsky's theorem on the sphere | 5 | Geometry
W: Monsky proved a square cannot be cut into an odd number of triangles of equal area. His proof is algebraic and uses straight-line coordinates, which a sphere does not have. Can a square drawn on a sphere, with four equal great-circle sides and four equal angles, be cut into an odd number of great-circle triangles of equal area?
M: For $0<A<2\pi$ let $Q_A\subset S^2$ be the regular spherical quadrilateral of area $A$. Let $\mathcal D(A)$ be the set of $n$ such that $Q_A$ can be dissected into $n$ geodesic triangles of equal area (vertices allowed anywhere, triangles with pairwise disjoint interiors covering $Q_A$). Does $\mathcal D(A)$ contain an odd number for some $A$? For all $A$? Determine $\mathcal D(A)$ as a function of $A$, and answer the same for regular quadrilaterals in the hyperbolic plane. In the Euclidean limit $A\to0$ only even $n$ occur (Monsky), and the 2-adic valuation argument has no spherical counterpart because spherical area is the angle excess rather than a polynomial in the vertices.
